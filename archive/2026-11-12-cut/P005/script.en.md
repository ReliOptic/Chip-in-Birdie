## 0:00–0:30 — The fire

On a Friday in March 2021, a machine caught fire inside a chip factory north of Tokyo. [C01][C04]

The burned area was about six hundred square metres. Two tennis courts. Five percent of the room. [C02]

One month later, the factory was running again. [C05]

And car companies still had no chips.

Not for another two months.

So here's the question. If the factory is fixed, where are the chips?

## 0:30–2:00 — Why this factory

The company is Renesas. You have probably never bought anything with its name on it. You have almost certainly ridden in something that depends on it.

Renesas makes microcontrollers. Small, cheap chips that do one job each. One runs the window. One runs the brakes. One watches the airbag. A modern car carries dozens.

In 2021, roughly one in three of those car microcontrollers, worldwide, came from Renesas. [C09]

Now picture a parts buyer at a car company that spring. She already has a problem. A year earlier, in the pandemic, carmakers cut their chip orders. Chip factories gave that space to phones and laptops. When car demand came back, the space was gone. [C16]

So she is already short. And then her phone lights up. Naka is on fire.

The first reports sound manageable. Small area. Nobody hurt. Twenty-three machines damaged. [C03]

Renesas says: we restart in a month. [C05]

A month. She can survive a month.

Then comes the second number. Full shipments: about a hundred days. [C06]

Same fire. Same factory. Two very different numbers. Both of them true.

## 2:00–4:30 — Three calendars

To see why, stop thinking of a chip factory as a machine that makes chips. Think of it as a very long line.

A blank silicon wafer goes in at one end. It visits hundreds of steps. A layer goes on, a pattern is printed, something is etched away, and again, and again. It does not come out that afternoon. It comes out about three months later. [C13]

So on any given day, the factory is full of wafers at every stage. Some went in last week. Some are almost done.

Now light the fire.

**Calendar one is the machines.** Burned tools are hauled out. The room is scrubbed, because smoke leaves particles, and one particle can kill a chip. New tools are ordered, shipped, installed, tuned. For Naka, the room was clean and the first tools were running in about a month. That is the number in the headline: production restarted. [C05]

**Calendar two is the wafers.** Restarted does not mean full. In the first weeks only part of the line runs. By June, about eighty-eight percent. [C07] And every wafer that goes in on restart day still has the whole line ahead of it. Three months of line. [C13]

What about the wafers that were already inside when the fire started? Some sat in smoke. Some sat half-finished for weeks. Each batch has to be judged: keep it, rework it, or scrap it.

**Calendar three is the customer.** A finished wafer still isn't a part in a car. It gets cut, packaged, tested, shipped, then built into a module by another company, then shipped again.

Put the three side by side.

Machines: fixed in a month.

Wafers: flowing at full rate around month three. [C08]

Customer: that is where the hundred days comes from. [C06]

In between, there is a hole. Renesas said so in advance: about a month in which nothing ships from this factory at all. [C06]

Nobody was hiding anything. "The factory is back" was true on calendar one. Our buyer lives on calendar three.

## 4:30–6:30 — The choices

Fine. So make the chips somewhere else for a while.

They tried. Renesas asked other chipmakers to build parts on its behalf. [C12] But a car chip isn't a generic part. It is approved for one factory, one process, sometimes one set of machines. Move it, and the carmaker wants it proven all over again: heat, cold, vibration, years of simulated life. That takes months too. [C14]

So the second factory doesn't save you this year. It saves you next time, if you paid for it beforehand.

Which leaves one option: chips you already have on a shelf.

And here the industry had made a choice, long before 2021. Car companies are famous for holding almost no inventory. Parts arrive just as the line needs them. Less cash sitting in boxes. Less warehouse. It is one of the great efficiency ideas of the last century.

It also means that when a supplier stops, you stop soon after.

But not everybody was in the same position. Because this had happened before. Same factory. In 2011, the earthquake shut Naka down for about three months. [C11]

Some companies treated that as bad luck. At least one treated it as a lesson, and started asking suppliers to hold months of chips. [C15]

So in March 2021, inside Toyota, an executive could say something most rivals couldn't: our chips will probably last until June. After July, we have a problem. [C10]

Read that again. The fire is in March. The factory restarts in April. And the company that prepared best is worried about July.

That is calendar three. Even the buffer was sized in months.

And the buffer wasn't free. Those chips cost money years before they were needed. For ten years it looked like waste. For one quarter it looked like genius. Both views were reasonable. They were just looking at different years.

## 6:30–8:00 — When the calendars meet

By early summer, the lines converge.

The replacement machines arrive at the end of May. [C07] The line climbs back to full rate. [C08] The wafers that started in April begin to come out the far end. In July, shipments are back where they were. [C08]

Three and a half months, for a fire that burned five percent of one room.

And it still didn't end the shortage. Naka was one hole in a system that was already short. [C16] Carmakers kept cutting production for the rest of the year.

So what did the fire actually show?

Not that chip factories are fragile. This one recovered faster than it first promised.

It showed that "recovered" has no single date. The fire crew has one. The factory manager has one. The buyer has one. They are months apart, and each of them is right.

## 8:00–8:30 — What to ask next time

Next time a headline says a chip factory is back online, ask one thing. Back on whose calendar?

And there's a detail we skipped. Even before the fire, Renesas didn't make most of its car chips itself. It handed them to another company's factory. [C12]

A company that makes chips for everyone and sells none of its own. Why would anyone trust a factory like that with their designs?

That's the next one.
